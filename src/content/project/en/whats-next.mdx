## When the demonstration is over, what remains?

A family member shows someone how to complete a task on their phone. It makes sense in the moment—but the next time, the sequence is difficult to reconstruct. The person who explained it may no longer be available.

**What's Next?** explores how this moment of assistance could remain useful. The concept proposes saving a demonstration of a specific app task, then making the gestures behind each screen change visible when the learner returns to it.

> Not just what happens on screen, but how to make it happen.

The original context is older adults learning smartphone applications in China, with relatives helping them. The aim is not to replace that relationship, but to give the learner a resource they can revisit independently.

## Choosing a learning challenge

The proposal considers several potential barriers: small visual elements, hard-to-hear guidance, fine-motor demands, and difficulty remembering an unfamiliar sequence. It narrows the design focus to **recalling a multi-step operation after being shown how to do it**.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/whats-next/user-barrier-diagram.webp" alt="Original concept illustration of potential hearing, vision, learning and finger-mobility barriers" caption="The original barrier illustration frames the design space. These are potential difficulties, not a profile shared by all older adults." />

The selected scenario is repeated instruction: a grandfather asks his younger relative to show him the same task again. The issue is not simply access to a phone, but how to recover the steps later without depending on another live explanation.

## A personal library of reusable guides

Rather than send a generic tutorial, a helper would record the exact operation the learner needs—for example, how to post to WeChat Moments. That demonstration would be saved under **an application and a named action**.

The relationship is simple: **app → task → demonstration**, with an optional voice note. One app could contain several task guides; a task would keep its explanation and recording together.

## Making the missing gesture visible

A screen recording shows an interface changing. It may not reveal what the demonstrator's finger did to produce that change: where it touched, how long it stayed, or in which direction it moved.

What's Next? proposes a visual layer that reconnects the screen to the action. In the WeChat example, a highlighted region and a hand cue indicate the intended interaction area.

<div className="case-grid">
<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/whats-next/gesture-replay-example.webp" alt="Original WeChat Discover mockup with a pink target highlight and a finger cue over the interaction area" caption="Gesture-enhanced replay concept: the screen, target region, and action cue are shown together." />
<section>
<h3>Three parts of a readable action</h3>
<p><strong>The screen</strong> preserves the interface seen during the demonstration.</p>
<p><strong>The target highlight</strong> identifies the region to attend to or interact with.</p>
<p><strong>The gesture cue</strong> indicates the kind or direction of action associated with that region.</p>
<p>For this to become a useful replay, the cues would need to match both the screen position and the moment in the demonstration. A static legend alone would not communicate the sequence.</p>
<h3>A hypothesis, not a result</h3>
<p>The original project calls this mechanism <em>Hypothetical Technology</em>. Its proposition is that visible gestures could help learners recall the teaching situation; the supplied materials do not establish that this effect has been tested.</p>
</section>
</div>

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/whats-next/gesture-reference-six.webp" alt="Six original gesture drawings labelled Tab, 2x Room In, Rotate, Touch and hold, Horizontal scroll, and Vertical scroll" caption="Original gesture vocabulary, retained as drawn. “Tab” and “2x Room In” are ambiguous source labels; their intended terminology has not been confirmed." />

The reference includes touch-and-hold, rotation, and horizontal and vertical scrolling. The two uncertain labels are not reinterpreted here as a particular double-tap or pinch gesture.

The design contribution is the relationship between **recorded interface, visible action, and later recall**—not a claim to have solved native touch-event capture.

### 3. The learner returns independently

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/whats-next/storyboard-03-complete.webp" alt="Original storyboard excerpt showing the grandfather reviewing a saved guide while Jack is busy" caption="When Jack is unavailable, Grandpa can return to the saved tutorial." />

The intended shift is from repeated dependence on immediate help to **reusable, personally relevant assistance**. Whether that actually improves independent task completion remains a question for future evaluation.

## From concept to a testable experience

The original work establishes an interaction concept, interface mockups, and a scenario—not a released application or validated recording system.

A useful next step would be a small playback prototype: a short demonstration with **manually authored, synchronised gesture cues**, step navigation, and optional spoken notes. It could explore whether the cues help a learner reconstruct the procedure without another live explanation.

That would test the **learning experience**, not native cross-app capture. Recording other apps and their touch events would remain a separate technical and privacy challenge. No functional prototype or live recording is claimed on this page.

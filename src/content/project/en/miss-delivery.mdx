## Delivery should fit a life—not interrupt it

You choose a delivery window. Then a meeting runs late, or your plans change. With groceries, a missed handover is more than an inconvenience: the food still needs appropriate storage, while the logistics operator faces another handling and delivery cycle.

**Miss Delivery** proposes a different response: help people plan for a delivery, but also give them an alternative when being at home becomes impossible. A nearby helper could receive and temporarily store the order, with the option of bringing it to the customer later.

The project was developed in **2019 for a Leadership & Innovation course brief concerning STEF**, a temperature-controlled logistics operator exploring B2C home delivery. It connects an app experience to a neighbourhood service network and the operational changes needed behind it.

### From logistics efficiency to customer flexibility

The team's preliminary analysis combined market trends, customer needs, competitor strategies and a SWOT assessment of the proposed B2C transition. The important tension was between an established logistics system and the uncertainty of individual customers' routines.

Competitor approaches were grouped into **prevention, cure and supporting information**. Time windows and tracking help people prepare, but do not resolve what happens after their availability changes. That gap became the opportunity for the proposal.

## The service behind the screens

Changing an address is easy to draw and difficult to operate. The proposal therefore connects the visible interactions to three backstage requirements.

**Match capacity, not just proximity.** Standardised parcel sizes would allow an order's chilled and frozen storage needs to be compared with the capacity declared by a helper. This would require coordination with the grocery partners' packaging process.

**Update one shared order.** Once a helper accepts, the destination and delivery status would need to update across the app and the logistics operator's systems. Customer and helper would retain a direct contact route.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/miss-delivery/shared-order.webp" className="case-pair-reference-size" background="white" alt="Paired original customer and helper mockups after acceptance, with the revised destination, tracking and call controls" caption="One arrangement, two perspectives: both sides need to know where the parcel is going and how to contact each other." />

**Reconsider the route.** The team proposed “double routing” within each delivery area: first serve customers retaining their original address, then use a second part of the hour for redirected helper deliveries. Keeping helpers within the same area was intended to contain the disruption.

This is an operational hypothesis, not a demonstrated routing improvement. Its feasibility depends on request timing, local helper density, route capacity and the reliability of the information exchanged.

## Trust belongs in the service, not just the profile

The final report refines the early rating idea into feedback about **communication, punctuality and, for helpers, parcel condition**. Both parties would see each other's feedback only after both had submitted it, with the aim of reducing retaliatory ratings.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/miss-delivery/mutual-ratings.webp" className="case-pair-reference-size" background="white" alt="Final report's two rating mockups showing communication and punctuality for both parties and parcel condition for the helper" caption="A later refinement: evaluate the service interaction rather than rely only on an undifferentiated star score." />

Ratings alone cannot establish food safety or resolve a failed handover. The original proposal identifies opportunistic behaviour, limited data and organisational inertia as risks. Before a pilot, the service would also need explicit arrangements for storage verification, accountability, disputes and an unavailable helper. These remain open requirements, not solved features.

## Making the business logic visible

The commercial proposition connects three kinds of value: **customers gain flexibility; helpers earn for receiving and storing orders; the operator could avoid some repeat-delivery costs**. The business model depends on making all three work together.

Named companies are part of the proposal's partner context, not evidence of new partnerships secured by the team." />

<div className="case-grid">
<section>
<h3>Build and sustain the network</h3>
<p>The 2019 model estimates <strong>€100,350 in initial investment</strong> across app development, systems integration, training and launch marketing. Ongoing costs cover maintenance and community growth.</p>
<p>Recruiting users is not enough: helper availability must grow in the same places and at useful times.</p>
</section>
<section>
<h3>Separate revenue from savings</h3>
<p>The proposal combines partner advertising and a proposed <strong>10% transaction commission from year three</strong> with potential savings in transport, handling, fuel and refrigeration.</p>
<p>Those savings depend on actually preventing repeat attempts—not merely recording more app activity.</p>
</section>
</div>

The original five-year financial model is an **exploratory 2019 estimate, not realised revenue or verified return on investment**. A pilot would need to test demand, willingness to pay, helper participation and operational costs before its projections could support an investment decision.

## A shared proposal, brought into focus

I worked as **team leader and design synthesiser**, helping connect different perspectives and make the team's proposal visible. The work brought together architecture, interior design, civil engineering and management engineering; the service concept and business case were developed collaboratively.

The most useful shift in this project was from treating missed delivery as a scheduling failure to treating it as a **service that needs to accommodate changing plans**. That reframing also made the limits clearer: a convincing interface is only one part of a viable service. The neighbourhood relationships, cold-chain responsibilities and logistics system have to support the same promise.

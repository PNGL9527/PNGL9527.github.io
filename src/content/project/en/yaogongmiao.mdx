## Reading an urban village through everyday life

Urban villages are often described through inadequate infrastructure, overcrowding, and redevelopment pressure. Yet they also sustain affordable housing, local commerce, and forms of social life that are difficult to read from a conventional land-use plan.

This 2017 investigation approached **Yaogongmiao as a lived environment, rather than only a redevelopment problem**. Combining architectural and anthropological approaches, it examined how residents, small businesses, and the built environment continually adapt to one another—from the wider city to a shop threshold or shared residential corridor.

The central question was how apparently fragmented spaces support a recognizable everyday order. The resulting maps and typological drawings make that order visible; they document existing conditions rather than propose a new masterplan.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/yaogongmiao/location-anhui-hefei-yaogongmiao.webp" alt="Location diagram moving from Anhui Province and Hefei to the outlined Yaogongmiao site" caption="Anhui → Hefei → Yaogongmiao. The village occupies a central position within the city." />

Yaogongmiao carries traces of Hefei's former outskirts within a rapidly transformed urban fabric. The warmth of everyday activity—*yanhuoqi*—is part of this setting, alongside the economic and spatial pressures around it.

## From rural settlement to urban enclave

The historical account traces a rural settlement through collective village organization and into an urbanized community. The Yaogong Village Committee was established in 1961, overseeing thirteen natural villages and fifteen villager groups. Agriculture and collective enterprises anchored its earlier identity.

Residential blocks and modern infrastructure gradually changed this context. Large-scale demolition and redevelopment began in 2012; pockets of the earlier fabric remained when the study was conducted in 2017.

The formation logic connects four conditions. These are not isolated problems, but a way to understand how informal settlement and adaptation develop together.

## From field observation to spatial analysis

The study moved through **literature review → field observation → interviews → questionnaires → spatial mapping → typological analysis**. Reading the site through several methods connected social questions with measurable spatial arrangements.

Literature research brought together social context and cultural identity, site morphology, livelihood and economic transformation, and urban governance. On site, three representative settings at different spatial scales were selected for closer observation. User shadowing added a day-in-the-life perspective on a key stakeholder's experience.

Representative spaces were measured and documented through plans and axonometric drawings. The analytical sequence moves from **where activities occur**, to **how shared spaces work**, to **how individual businesses and homes are arranged**.

## Mapping overlap, not separate zones

The functional plans use a common site outline to compare main-street frontage, entertainment, catering, and living. Their value lies in the relationship between the layers: commercial activity penetrates the residential fabric rather than forming a completely separate district.

**Overlap and hybridity** are the key findings: ground-level spaces accommodate several uses, while thresholds and residual areas are reconfigured in response to demand. A land-use label alone cannot describe how these spaces operate.

The five-node study takes that observation further, examining how small public spaces mediate between housing, activity, and everyday logistics.

## Five ways shared space takes shape

The nodes are **readings of existing spatial conditions, not five new architectural proposals**. Together, they show how shared life depends on courtyards, corners, passages, terrain, and service spaces—not only on formally designated public facilities.

## Six storefront studies: commerce meets the street

The commercial strip serves students, families, older residents, and workers through a range of small, self-organized businesses. The six studies below compare the arrangement of operational space, customer space, and the street interface.

Each drawing pairs a plan with an exploded axonometric. Read together, they reveal recurring strategies—display spilling towards the sidewalk, preparation facing the street, outdoor dining, and commerce layered beneath other activities—without reducing the six cases to a single shop type.

## A spatial logic produced from within

What can appear disordered from outside becomes more legible when read through the people, activities, and adaptations that sustain it. Housing, commerce, circulation, and shared space do not simply sit beside one another: they continually reshape one another.

> The urban village is not only a dense settlement morphology. It is a socio-spatial system shaped through everyday practices, informal economies, and adaptive uses of space.

The contribution of this investigation is a way of seeing: moving between the urban context and small spatial arrangements reveals relationships that a redevelopment-only perspective can overlook.

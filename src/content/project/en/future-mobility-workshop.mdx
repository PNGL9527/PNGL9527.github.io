## Teaching beyond the vehicle

What changes when a shuttle bus no longer needs a driver? For design students, the answer can quickly become a new vehicle shape or another screen. The teaching challenge was to widen that frame: **who participates in this mobility system, what connects them, and whose needs might a proposed future overlook?**

Across **2022–2023**, I supported remote, project-based teaching within a final-year Product Design course at **Shandong University**. The workshops combined systemic and speculative design to help students move from understanding an existing system to proposing alternative futures for automated public mobility.

My role was **Teaching Assistant and Workshop Co-facilitator**, rather than sole course instructor. I supported the delivery of methods, supervised teams during research and concept development, and provided formative feedback on their reasoning, concept coherence and final communication. The framework was developed and applied by the teaching team; the design proposals shown here are student work.

The examples and cohort figures below document the **2022 workshop round**, subsequently discussed in two 2023 publications. They are not a combined headcount for both years.

## A shared scaffold, not a prescribed solution

The pedagogical intention was to build system understanding **before solution-making**, connect imagination to stakeholder relationships, and make reflection part of the design process rather than only a final report.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/future-mobility-workshop/teaching-process.webp" alt="Seven-stage teaching process from preparation and system exploration through possible futures, scenarios, reframing, ideation and iterative reflection, with feedback loops" caption="The teaching team's framework, adapted from Yan et al. (2023). The backward arrows are as important as the forward sequence." />

The documented round ran from **21 September to 30 October 2022**. Twenty-eight students worked in six teams across three parallel strands: **vehicle interior, exterior interaction and the station**. Each strand explored nearer- and longer-term futures, giving students a common process without requiring the same answer.

For remote delivery, explicit milestones and structured feedback helped connect these stages. My contribution was to support students in carrying earlier findings forward, rather than treating each method as an isolated exercise.

## Making relationships visible before imagining solutions

Students began by exploring perspectives beyond their own: passengers, service staff, people facing travel barriers, pedestrians and pets. Role-playing was used to temporarily step outside the designer's position; maps then made the relationships between those perspectives discussable.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/future-mobility-workshop/role-exploration.webp" alt="Student role-exploration graphic linking service personnel, riders, passers-by and pets around a mobility service" caption="Student role-exploration artwork connects people, service responsibilities and travel needs (Sustainability, Figure 4)." />

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/future-mobility-workshop/interior-stakeholder-map.webp" alt="Interior Group B student map connecting passengers, service providers, accessibility needs, pets and possible technologies" caption="Student work · Interior Group B. The map combines stakeholder relationships with candidate technologies (Sustainability, Figure 5)." />

The mapping exercise was not simply a list of users. It asked students to consider **how a service, technology or design decision changes another actor's experience**. In tutorials and reviews, I supported teams in making these connections explicit and strengthening the rationale behind their concepts.

These artefacts also gave us a shared reference in remote discussion: relationships that were visible on a map could be revisited when a later proposal became disconnected from the original investigation.

## Imagining futures—and questioning their assumptions

Near-term foresight and longer-term speculation created different design demands. The horizons below are relative to the original workshop, **not forecasts made today**.

<p>Students worked with recognisable technologies and current practices. The station-focused paper expands this horizon to 5–10 years. The challenge was to distinguish a plausible development from an unsupported technological leap.</p>
</section>
<section>
<h3>Distant future · around +50 years</h3>
<p>Students could explore more radical social and technological change. The challenge was not to predict the future, but to examine what their imagined world assumed, enabled or excluded.</p>

Backcasting connected the imagined situation to the present and helped reframe the problem. Teams then created concept libraries before selecting ideas for further development, sharing proposals and revising them through feedback.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/future-mobility-workshop/interior-concept-library.webp" alt="A photographed board of numbered student sketches exploring many possible interior design ideas" caption="Student work · Interior Group B. A concept library makes alternatives visible before selection (Sustainability, Figure 7)." />

The educational value lies in the transition from **“we can imagine this” to “we can explain why this proposal belongs in this scenario.”** My formative feedback focused on that coherence, the design rationale and the clarity of its communication.

## Student work: the station as a connected place

**Station Group A** illustrates how system exploration can develop into a service and spatial proposition. Instead of treating the station only as a place to board, the team connected mobility with historical and cultural experiences, green travel, animal protection and inclusive access.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/future-mobility-workshop/station-future-vision.webp" alt="Station Group A's future scenario map linking different themed stations, infrastructure, services and user activities" caption="Student work · Station Group A. The future scenario map broadens the station from a transport node into a mobility ecosystem (Sustainability, Figure 17; Applied Sciences, Figure 2)." />

### From a vision to relationships

The ecosystem map links the station to service providers, suppliers, public authorities, residents and passengers. It exposes the relationships a richer station experience would depend on, rather than treating each proposed feature as self-contained.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/future-mobility-workshop/station-ecosystem.webp" alt="Station Group A ecosystem map connecting service providers, station management, government, suppliers, residents, passengers and pets through several flows" caption="Student work · Station Group A. Ecosystem relationships developed through gigamapping (Applied Sciences, Figure 4)." />

### From relationships to a spatial proposal

The team separated a waiting area from an area for additional services and interaction. The proposal includes resting and wayfinding alongside themed displays and activities; the drawings translate the scenario into spatial arrangements and touchpoints.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/future-mobility-workshop/station-design-development.webp" alt="Student station sketches, plan and service illustrations distinguishing waiting Area A from interaction Area B" caption="Student work · Station Group A. Sketches and zoning explore how the proposed services occupy space (Sustainability, Figure 18)." />

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/future-mobility-workshop/station-design-proposal.webp" alt="Student rendering and orthographic views of a curved station with an exhibition area and passenger entrance" caption="Student work · Station Group A. A developed architectural concept, not a built station (Sustainability, Figure 19)." />

My teaching contribution was to help the team connect this vision to its earlier stakeholder and system exploration, question isolated ideas, and strengthen relationships among **services, spaces, technologies and users**. The student artefacts show that translation; they do not establish the proposal's performance in a real transport system.

## A contrasting future: making an experience legible

**Station Group B** explored a more distant future, with different assumptions about the environment, community life and technology. Its storyboard follows a person through finding a station, entering, boarding, travelling and using additional services.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/future-mobility-workshop/station-future-storyboard.webp" alt="Station Group B storyboard following a speculative passenger journey through navigation, boarding, travel and station services" caption="Student work · Station Group B. A storyboard turns an abstract worldview into a sequence that can be discussed (Sustainability, Figure 22)." />

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/future-mobility-workshop/station-hmi-scenarios.webp" alt="Four student HMI scenario panels pairing speculative interface views with corresponding station interactions" caption="Student work · Station Group B. Interface scenarios connect proposed information and interaction to moments in the journey (Sustainability, Figure 23)." />

In a teaching context, a sequence makes it possible to ask where information is needed, which actions the system assumes, and how the person understands what happens next. The work is shown as **a speculative student proposal**, not a prediction or an implemented HMI. Group A and Group B are alternative explorations, not successive versions of one design.

## What the work reveals about learning

The selected work makes three aspects of the learning process visible: students connected multiple actors within a system, used scenarios to question the conventional station, and translated those investigations into spatial, service and interaction opportunities.

The publications add a second perspective. The **Sustainability** study collected questionnaires from all 28 participants in the documented round; the **Applied Sciences** paper reports interviews with five participants. Their feedback discusses the usefulness of role exploration and mapping, alongside difficulties with backcasting and the knowledge needed to reason across a complex system.

That is **reflective feedback, not evidence of a measured learning gain or a controlled comparison between teaching methods**. The proposals also remain outside a real shuttle deployment, limiting what can be concluded about their practical effectiveness.

For me, the central teaching lesson was that future-oriented work becomes more coherent when imagination has a visible foundation. System maps provide that foundation; tutorials and reviews help students return to it as their ideas develop.

In a future iteration, I would make intended learning outcomes and review criteria more explicit from the outset, and add a structured peer-critique session **between scenario development and service ideation**. This would help students examine each other's assumptions before investing in a polished final proposal.

## Teaching, student authorship and related research

This page presents **my teaching assistance and co-facilitation**, not sole authorship of the course or its student designs. The framework is teaching-team work; maps, sketches, storyboards, interfaces and renders are credited to their respective student teams.

The related research was developed collaboratively with colleagues at Politecnico di Milano. The papers acknowledge Professor **Xiaofeng Xie's** support at Shandong University. My publication contributions include investigation and writing, and, in the Applied Sciences paper, methodology and formal analysis; these research credits are distinct from authorship of student work.

## From experience to idea

A driving alert can feel like a helpful companion—or an irritating interruption. Describing its function alone misses how someone experiences the relationship.

Thing-sona grew from interviews with 12 non-professional drivers in China and Italy, within my [co-driving research](/portfolio/co-driving-pleasure/). Their accounts suggested a vocabulary of roles and qualities: the same lane-departure warning could be understood as a **Helper** or a **Fuss**.

The design question became: **how can people express these relationships in their own terms?** Persona thinking and the adjective–noun title combinations in *Splatoon 3* inspired a simple pairing mechanism, grounded in a person's situation rather than a technology's supposed personality.

## A language of Who + How

The toolkit combines two decks: blue **Who** cards suggest a perceived role; red **How** cards describe its quality. A pairing becomes the beginning of a story, not a fixed classification.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/thing-sona/Who.png" alt="Complete blue Who sheet: Whistleblower, Burden-reducer, Secretary, Truble-maker, Friend and Fuss, with all six cards and their descriptions visible" caption="Who — the blue deck offers a starting vocabulary of perceived relational roles." />

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/thing-sona/How.png" alt="Complete red How sheet: Dependent, Reliable, Opinioned, Precise, Managable and Prudent, with all six cards and their descriptions visible" caption="How — the red deck offers qualities that can be paired with those roles. Original card wording is retained." />

For example, **Dependent + Secretary** invites someone to explain what they rely on and why. The meaning can change with the person, technology, or situation. Blank cards keep that vocabulary open: participants can draw and name a relation that the existing deck does not capture.

## Put the relationship in context

The board connects each pairing to a technology, a person, and a situation. Separate areas for expectations and experience help make differences discussable.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/thing-sona/context-board-clean.webp" background="white" alt="Original Thing-sona board with technology, participant and driving context, plus Expected and Real relation areas and notes" caption="The original paper board: describe the context, compose relations, and explain the contrast." />

**Situate → Pair → Compare → Reflect.** Describe what matters, choose Who and How cards, and add a short explanation. The goal is a conversation about the relation—not a score for the technology.

The web prototype carries this process into an editable workspace, with searchable cards, custom-card creation, and project export/import.

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/thing-sona/prototype-workspace.png" alt="Thing-sona web prototype with contextual information, a card library, and Expected and Experienced relation areas" caption="The web prototype. This built-in AI-writing example is illustrative, not an interview record." />

In that example, a writer expects a **Dependable Helper** but experiences an **Opinionated Substitute**. The contrast opens a question: when does assistance support a person's voice, and when does it displace it?

<LightboxImage src="/assets/projects/thing-sona/prototype-custom-card.png" alt="Thing-sona custom-card interface with card type, title, meaning, and illustration selection" caption="Custom-card creation carries the open vocabulary into the digital prototype." />

## What this opens up

Early discussions with nine automotive design practitioners suggested value for inquiry and concept positioning, while raising questions about fitting the toolkit into existing workflows. These were preliminary impressions of the early toolkit, not a validation of the current web prototype.

Thing-sona offers a way to make relationships discussable. The next step is to understand how people reinterpret its vocabulary across technologies and design situations.
